01

Why Train in Fewer Bits

Every number in the site’s trainer is a 64-bit double. Real training uses 16-bit and even 8-bit numbers, because they halve or quarter the memory and the traffic, and the matrix units run several times faster on them.

Memory

Weights, gradients and activations at 2 bytes instead of 4 (deck 09).

Traffic

Two bytes per parameter per worker per step is what crosses the network when workers exchange BF16 gradients (deck 10, deck 12).

The cost

Precision and range: small numbers fall off the bottom of the format and become zero; large ones overflow.

02

Formats: Exponent Against Mantissa

A floating-point number has a sign, an exponent (its range) and a mantissa (its precision: how many values per factor of two). With \(e\) exponent and \(m\) mantissa bits and bias \(2^{e-1}-1\), the spacing near \(|x|\) is \(2^{\max(\lfloor\log_2|x|\rfloor,\,1-\text{bias}) - m}\), so the relative rounding error is at most \(2^{-m-1}\).

BF16 (bfloat16) is 2 bytes like FP16 but keeps FP32’s 8-bit exponent: the same range as FP32 and about 2–3 significant decimal digits (Kalamkar et al. 2019). FP16 has more precision but a narrow range.

BF16’s range keeps every gradient; FP16 loses the smallest; FP8 loses many. That trade is why most large runs today compute in BF16: it needs no loss scaling. Multiplying the loss by \(2^k\) before the backward pass shifts every gradient up by \(k\) binades, into FP16’s range:

05

Mixed Precision: Master Weights and Loss Scaling

Micikevicius et al. (2018) made 16-bit training work with three ideas:

FP32 master copy

Compute in 16 bits, but the optimiser updates an FP32 copy of the weights. An update is often smaller than the spacing between 16-bit values near the weight, so adding it to a 16-bit weight would do nothing.

FP32 accumulation

The matrix products accumulate their sums in FP32, so long dot products do not lose their small terms.

Loss scaling

\(\tilde L = 2^k L \Rightarrow \tilde g = 2^k g\); the optimiser divides by \(2^k\) in FP32. Dynamic scaling halves the scale and skips the step on an overflow, and doubles it after a long run of good steps (PyTorch’s GradScaler).

A 5,376-parameter model trained for 2,000 steps is forgiving; at scale, with longer runs and smaller late learning rates, the same losses accumulate, which is why master copies and loss scaling are standard.

07

FP8 and Per-Tensor Scaling

The newest accelerators also multiply in 8-bit floats. Micikevicius et al. (2022) recommend E4M3 (largest 448) for weights and activations and E5M2 (more range, less precision) for gradients, with a scaling factor per tensor so each tensor’s largest value lands near the top of the format:

$$s = 2^{\lfloor \log_2(\max_{\text{fmt}} / \operatorname{amax}(x)) \rfloor}, \qquad \hat x = \operatorname{round}(s x)/s$$

No scaling

Never leaves the plateau: 2.957 ± 0.011, with 16% of gradient values flushed to zero.

Per-tensor scaling

Two of three seeds train (2.383, 2.965, 2.374): three mantissa bits are coarse, and at this scale whether the run escapes the plateau is a coin toss.

In the emulation only what the matrix products read is FP8; what they write stays BF16, as in those recipes. The site’s notebook 09 trains a 60-million-parameter model in each format on a GPU: GPU run pending, so no GPU number appears here.

08

Quantised Gradients

Beyond floating point, values can be quantised to a few bits of integer: pick a full scale \(F\), divide it into \(n = 2^{b-1}-1\) steps each side of zero, round each value to the nearest step, and clip anything beyond the full scale.

$$\hat x = \Delta \cdot \operatorname{clip}\big(\operatorname{round}(x/\Delta), -n, n\big), \qquad \Delta = F/n$$

Every weight gradient quantised per tensor (full scale = its largest value) before clipping and the optimiser step; three seeds (results.md §9b). Adam is forgiving here too, since it normalises each weight’s step (Alistarh et al. 2016 analyse quantised SGD). Quantised gradients are what compressed distributed training sends between workers (deck 12), and analogue or optical hardware imposes the same quantisation on whatever passes through it.

09

What to Take Away

  • Exponent bits buy range, mantissa bits precision. BF16 = FP32’s range in 2 bytes, so no loss scaling.
  • 16-bit training = 16-bit compute + FP32 master weights + FP32 accumulation (+ loss scaling for FP16).
  • FP8 needs per-tensor scaling: E4M3 forward, E5M2 for gradients.
  • On this toy every reasonable recipe lands within the seed spread; the safeguards matter at scale.
